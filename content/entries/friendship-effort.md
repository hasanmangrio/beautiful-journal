For most of my twenties I was what you'd call a planner. I sent the first text, suggested the date, found the restaurant, made the reservation. In most of my friendships, I was the one who made things happen.

For most of that time, I resented it a little.

Not loudly — I never complained about it directly or kept score in any conscious way. But somewhere underneath the planning there was this low-grade feeling of imbalance. Why do I always have to be the one? Don't they want to see me?

I've changed my mind about this recently.

## The Thing About Effort

Here's what I missed: effort is a love language, but it's not a universal one. And the absence of effort doesn't mean the absence of feeling.

Some people are bad at initiating not because they don't care, but because initiating costs them something it doesn't cost me. Maybe they're anxious about whether they'd be welcome. Maybe they've been burned by cancellations and learned not to be the one who reaches out. Maybe they just don't think about social logistics the way I do — not better or worse, just differently.

I'm good at logistics. I like sending the first text. Making plans gives me something to look forward to. When I started thinking about it as a skill I happen to have, rather than a burden I happen to carry, it reframed everything.

I'm not doing the work for my friends. I'm doing the work *with* my friends, using the particular capacities I have.

## But Also

Some people really don't reciprocate. And that's different. There's a version of this where I'm not recognizing an imbalance that's real and worth addressing — where someone genuinely doesn't value the friendship as much as I do, and I keep initiating out of hope rather than joy.

I've had to learn to tell those apart. It's not always obvious. The way I try to check: do I feel better after spending time with this person? Does the investment feel worth it on its own terms, separate from whether they reciprocate exactly?

If yes, I plan. If the answer is increasingly no, I stop, and see what happens.

## What Actually Matters

I have a friend I've known for fifteen years who has never once, in that time, made a plan. I always text first. I always suggest the when and where.

She is also one of the most present people I know. When we're together she's entirely there — phone down, fully listening, asking the right questions, laughing the hard laugh. She remembers things I said months ago. She shows up when it actually matters.

She just doesn't make plans. I used to find this maddening. Now I think: we both do what we're good at. I am good at logistics. She is good at presence. Between the two of us, we manage to actually see each other, and the time is always worth it.

That's enough. That's more than enough.

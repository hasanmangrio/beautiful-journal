Two years ago I stopped reading. Not dramatically — I didn't throw my books away or make some announcement. I just... stopped picking them up. The pile on my nightstand kept growing and I kept not touching it, and eventually I moved the pile to a shelf and stopped thinking of it as something I was going to do soon.

Last month I picked one up. I don't know why that particular Tuesday. I think I was just tired of opening an app.

The book was *Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow* by Gabrielle Zevin, which I'd bought on a recommendation fourteen months prior and felt a little guilty about every time I walked past it. It took me about four chapters to get back into the rhythm of reading — to stop looking for the scroll, to stop expecting the text to do something. And then I read for three hours without noticing.

I finished it in a week. Then started another one.

## What I'd Missed

There's something reading does that nothing else really replicates — it's the only medium where someone else's interiority becomes yours. You don't observe the character's thoughts; you think them. You don't watch someone fall in love; you fall in love alongside them, using your own emotional memory to fill in all the parts the author leaves blank.

That's a strange and remarkable thing. And I'd been going without it for two years.

I'd been watching things, which is good. I'd been listening to podcasts and music and conversations. But none of it gave me access to someone's inner life in quite the same way. The closest is maybe a really good essay — but even that is the writer presenting their interiority, not inviting you inside it.

## On Attention

Reading also, it turns out, fixed something I didn't realize was broken: my attention span.

I'd noticed it getting worse but had sort of accepted it as a fact about modern life. Everything demanding a piece of you, every notification a tiny claim on your focus. I thought I'd just become a person who needed things to move fast.

But a month of reading for an hour before bed has done something measurable. I'm better at conversations. I'm less likely to reach for my phone when I'm slightly bored. I can sit through a long film without checking the time.

It's not magic and it's not a productivity hack. It's just that attention, like most things, responds to practice.

## The Pile

The nightstand pile is growing again. I let it. I've stopped thinking of it as failure and started thinking of it as wealth — these are all the things I'm going to feel, when I get to them.

That feels better.
